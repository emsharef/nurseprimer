Last night's tape is still pinned to the noticeboard, URGENT in Wick's square capitals, next to the felt-tip
one. On the Ward Board, one-rule Wick sits a whisker above *Everything can wait*.

You come in early with a brass card drawer from the back of the supply cupboard, the kind that used to hold the
ward's index of patients, and fit it into Wick's side where a panel was missing. One card wasn't enough. Tonight
Wick gets a whole drawer of them.

"A rulebook," says Bea. She sets her tea down. "Go on, then. Write down what I know."

The rules go in a little language Wick can follow. Each one starts with *if*, names some words in quotes, and
ends with an arrow and a verdict:

```
if "glass" and "dont" -> urgent
if any of "fit", "floppy" -> urgent
if "rash" and not "fade" -> urgent
```

Wick reads the drawer from the front. The first rule that matches decides, and if none does, the last line
answers. A word in quotes matches a whole word, so *"purple"* doesn't match *purplish* unless you write
*"purpl\*"*. Apostrophes are dropped, so *don't* is *dont*. That's the whole language.

The drawer starts with last night's one rule. Bea has three patches ready for you, on index cards, and each new
card goes in at the front, so Wick reads it first.

The felt-tip card is free: five messages fixed and nothing broken. Enjoy it, because it's the last patch that
comes free.

The glass card fixes twenty-three emergencies and breaks ten calm messages. Read the ones it broke. A grandparent
asks how much medicine to give because *I dont want to get it wrong*, and mentions that the rash on her
granddaughter's arms goes pale under a glass. *Glass*, and *dont*: both present and correct. Wick has no idea the
*don't* belongs to the medicine. Bea's third card, for fits and floppiness, fixes thirteen and breaks six, and
every one of the six is a parent writing that there has been *no fit*, or that the child is *not floppy*.

Now write your own. Bea's challenge is in the panel. Look through the emergencies Wick misses, find a phrase that
catches some, and watch what it breaks. Open *Which rules did the work* to see each rule's record.

<details class="hint">
  <summary>Stuck? Some phrases from the pile.</summary>

  Parents write *stiff neck*, but also *won't put her chin down*. They write *floppy*, *hard to wake* and *not
  answering me properly*. Breathing is *fast*, *grunting*, *sucking in under his ribs*. A cry is *high pitched* or
  *thin*. Skin is *mottled*, *blotchy*, *grey*; nappies are *dry*. Each of those is a sign in the NHS guidance the
  labels follow, and each can be written a dozen other ways.
</details>

When you're happy with it, or tired of it, put your rulebook on the Ward Board.

## Three o'clock

You don't stop. Every time Wick misses an emergency you write a rule for it, and every time a rule sends an
ambulance to a child with a bit of blackcurrant on her chin you write another rule above it. Rules for babies
under three months (Wick can't read a temperature, so you list *38*, *39* and *40* as words). Rules for *mottled*
and *bluish* and *bone dry*. A rule for anyone who has already typed the word *ambulance*.

<Plate id="ch02-three" size="column" />

By three o'clock the drawer holds forty-one cards, and on the Day Shift Wick is better than it has ever been:
96% right, three missed emergencies, thirteen needless alarms. You have read most of those 395 messages twice.

Bea reads the Day Shift numbers over your shoulder. Then she goes to the cabinet and comes back with a different
tray. "The Night Shift," she says. "Two clinics we've never had messages from. Voice-to-text, a lot of it. Plenty of
families writing in two languages at once." She puts it down in front of Wick. "You didn't write your rules
for these."

Switch to the Night Shift and open the missed emergencies. Fifteen of them, where the Day Shift had three. Read
them and they're not hard. A foster carer writes *petechiae*. A grandparent writes that the spots are
*non-blanching, it seems*. A childminder pressed a glass on a little girl's spots and *dem nuh fade*. A boy's neck
is *stif*. Another boy's hands and feet are *freezin cauld*. Any nurse on this line would have known each of them
for an emergency by the end of the first sentence. Wick has cards for *dont fade* and *didnt go* and *still
there*, and none of these families said it the way the cards do.

Put the three o'clock rulebook on the board next to yours, then switch the board to the Night Shift.

<WardBoard />

People who build machines call this *brittle*. A rulebook works exactly as far as its author thought ahead, and
stops dead one word past it. Every patch is written for the messages you've seen, so it sits a little better on
the Day Shift and is no help at all to the next new word. You can always add one more card, and there is always
one more word.

## Ten past three

At ten past three a message comes in from a mother. You read it and your stomach turns over, and you feed it to
Wick anyway.

Wick knows *spots* and *dots* and *rash*. It has never met *speckly*. It prints CAN WAIT.

Try her message on your own rulebook too. If yours catches it, change one word, *speckly* to *freckly*, or *glass*
to *tumbler*, and try again.

You are already reaching for a blank card. Bea is reading the tape.
