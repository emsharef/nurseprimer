The rulebook didn't survive the last night. You had written forty-one rules for Wick, each one a patch on the
one before, and at ten past three a mother wrote that her son's legs had gone "all speckly". Wick knew *spots*
and *dots* and *rash*. It had never met *speckly*. It printed CAN WAIT.

Bea read the tape, folded it in half and dropped it in the bin. "You can't write down everything a nurse knows,"
she said. "I've been trying for thirty years."

Across the desk, Lyceum's speaking tube sits in its lacquered cabinet, silent and polished. Nobody wrote it a
rulebook either.

Tonight Bea comes back from the supply cupboard with a cardboard box and sets it down next to Wick. Inside is the
gutted shell of an old valve radio and a jam jar full of brass knobs.

"My father mended these," she says. "Nobody ever wrote a rule for how a radio should sound. You turn the knobs
until it sounds right."

## A knob for every word

You fit eight of them across Wick's chest with Bea's jeweller's screwdriver, one for each of eight words.

This Wick doesn't follow rules about what a message means. It only notices which words are in it. Turn a word's
knob to the right and, whenever that word turns up, it pushes Wick toward *urgent*. Turn it left and it pushes
toward *can wait*. Before it reads a single word, Wick already leans a little toward *can wait*, because most
messages can. It adds every push to that lean, and if the total crosses the middle, its eyes go red.

No grammar and no meaning: a row of knobs and a sum.

Bea watches you chase the numbers for a while. "Get it under fifty missed," she says, "and you can make the
tea."

When you get there, look at what you've won: forty-odd children whose parents wrote in frightened, and Wick still
says they can wait. If your *glass* knob is up, look at the grandmother in the tray, the one whose granddaughter's
rash goes white under the glass. Your best knob sends her an ambulance.

"That's your rulebook again," says Bea. "Every turn mends one message and breaks another." And you have eight
knobs. The messages in her pile use nearly two thousand different words more than once.

## Wick turns its own knobs

"So stop turning them," says Bea. "Let the thing do it."

She has you give Wick a knob for every one of those two thousand words, all set to zero. Then she lifts the Day
Shift messages off the top of the pile and locks them in the drawer. "For marking it afterwards. It doesn't get to
see the exam."

What's left, about sixteen hundred messages, each already marked *urgent* or *can wait*, goes to Wick. It reads
every one and gives its answer, along with how sure it is: the further its total lands past the middle, the surer
it is. Each answer earns a wrongness score: almost nothing if Wick was sure and right, a little if it hedged, a lot
if it was sure and wrong. Add them up and you have one number for how wrong Wick is about the whole pile.

Now, for every knob, ask one question: *if I turned this a hair to the right, would that number go up or down?*
Turn it a hair whichever way helps, and a little further for the knobs that help most. All the knobs move at once.
Then Wick reads the whole pile again.

(Why not just count mistakes? Because a hair's turn almost never changes the count, so Wick couldn't tell which way
to go. The wrongness score moves a little with every hair.)

<Plate id="ch03-training" size="column" />

Picture the wrongness as a hillside in fog. Wick can't see the valley. It can only feel which way the ground tilts
under its feet and take a step that way. Near the bottom the ground levels out and the steps get small. People call
all this *training*, and the walk downhill *gradient descent*.<NoteRef id="gd" /> The engineers who installed the
speaking tube said Lyceum's machine was made the same way, only with billions of knobs.

The knobs on Wick's chest turn by themselves, a click at a time, and the tape curls off the desk. When it stops,
read the two lines under the chart: Wick's score on the locked-away Day Shift, and yours.

Then look at your eight knobs where Wick left them. *Purple*, the word your very first rule was built on, it has
barely touched: purple turns up in both piles, frightening and not, so it tells Wick almost nothing. The lists are
stranger still. *Rash* leans toward *can wait*, because most rashes parents write about are the harmless kind. Little
words like *it* and *just* lean calm too (look them up), because calm messages are full of "it goes pale when I
press it" and "I just wanted to check". Wick doesn't know what any word means. It only knows which pile each word
tended to turn up in.

Now type *sent*, *app* and *myriverside* into the lookup. All three are in Wick's top thirty, out of nearly two
thousand, nearly as urgent as *dots* and *wake*. They come from the line one clinic's phone app adds to the bottom of

Bea circles them on the printout and says nothing, which is worse.

Both urgent, and within a whisker of each other. Look at the word chips. Most of the push comes from the scene,
*glass*, *pressed*, *legs*, *purple*, and that's the same in both. Then there's *dont*, one of Wick's loudest alarm
words, third on its whole list. In the pile, every single message that said the spots "don't fade" or "don't go
away" was urgent, so Wick learned to flinch at *don't*, even though most of the time parents use it to say they
don't know or don't want to bother anyone. And Wick has no idea which word *don't* belongs to. Tipped out of the
bag, your two messages are the same handful of words, plus *worry*, which Wick hardly cares about.

You reach for the knobs. Bea puts her hand over them.

"They're as good as they'll ever be," she says. "It found the best settings there are, for a machine that sees a
message as a bag of loose words. Turn them all night. It still can't see which way round the words go."

**Your turn to break it.** Write a message that any nurse would call an emergency, but that Wick calls *can wait*.
(Hint: reassuring words are heavy.) Then try the opposite: a perfectly calm message that sends Wick into a panic.
Every one that fools it, keep: Bea starts a Rogues' Gallery in a shoebox under the desk, and every Wick you build
from now on has to face it.

<MathDrawer title="what Wick computes, and how it learns">
  Each word $w$ has a weight $\theta_w$ (its knob) and Wick has a bias $b$ (the starting lean). For a message $x$,
  let $W(x)$ be the set of distinct words in it. Wick's score and its confidence that the message is urgent are

  $$
  s(x) = b + \sum_{w \in W(x)} \theta_w, \qquad p(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-s(x)}}.
  $$

  For a message with label $y$ ($1$ for urgent, $0$ for can wait), the wrongness is the log loss
  $\ell = -\,[\,y \log p + (1-y)\log(1-p)\,]$. Its slope with respect to each knob is simple: for every word in the
  message, $\partial \ell / \partial \theta_w = p - y$. Training averages this over all $N$ training messages and
  takes a step of size $\eta$ against it:

  $$
  \theta_w \leftarrow \theta_w - \eta \left( \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i:\, w \in W(x_i)} \big(p(x_i) - y_i\big) + \lambda\,\theta_w \right),
  $$

  and the lean moves the same way, summed over every message:

  $$
  b \leftarrow b - \frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{i} \big(p(x_i) - y_i\big).
  $$

  This is plain (full-batch) gradient descent on logistic regression. What it walks downhill on is the average
  wrongness plus a small penalty on large knobs (not the lean),

  $$
  \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \ell_i + \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_w \theta_w^2 ,
  $$

  which is what the chart plots. Here $\eta = 1$, $\lambda = 0.001$, three hundred steps, every knob starts at
  zero, and only words that appear in at least two training messages get a knob; a word with no knob adds zero.
</MathDrawer>

<Notes>
  <NoteDef id="gd">The knobs are usually called *weights* and the starting lean a *bias*; the whole machine is a
  *logistic regression*, a long-established tool in statistics. Machines that learn by adjusting weights go back at
  least to the perceptron<Cite k="rosenblatt1958" />, which used a simpler error-correcting rule rather than
  gradient descent. Here every step uses all the training messages at once and
  the same step size every time ("plain" gradient descent); real systems usually use small random batches and
  adaptive methods such as Adam.</NoteDef>
</Notes>
