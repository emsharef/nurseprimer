The rulebook didn't survive the last night. You had written forty-one rules for Wick, each one a patch on the
one before, and at ten past three a mother wrote that her son's legs had gone "all speckly". Wick knew *spots*
and *dots* and *rash*. It had never met *speckly*. It printed CAN WAIT.

Bea read the tape, folded it in half and dropped it in the bin. "You can't write down everything a nurse knows,"
she said. "I've been trying for thirty years."

Tonight she comes back from the supply cupboard with a cardboard box and sets it down next to Wick. Inside is the
gutted shell of an old valve radio and a jam jar full of brass knobs.

"My father mended these," she says. "Nobody ever wrote a rule for how a radio should sound. You turn the knobs
until it sounds right."

## A knob for every word

So here is the new Wick. It no longer tries to follow rules about what a message means. It only notices which
words are in it.

Every word gets a knob. Turn a word's knob to the right and that word nudges Wick toward *urgent*. Turn it left
and the word nudges Wick toward *can wait*. When a message arrives, Wick adds up the nudges from every word in it,
starting from a resting lean, and if the total tips past the middle its eyes go red.

That's all. No grammar, no meaning, no rulebook: a row of knobs and a sum. You've fitted the first eight.

You'll probably find that two or three knobs do most of the work, and that every turn fixes some messages and
breaks others. Even Bea's modest challenge takes some fiddling, and when you meet it, look at the count of missed
emergencies again. It's still dozens. *Glass* is a strong one: parents mostly mention the glass test when a rash has frightened them.
*Fade* is treacherous, because it turns up in "they don't fade" and in "they fade fine". And however clever you
are, you have eight knobs. Tonight's messages contain more than three thousand different words.

## Wick turns its own knobs

"So stop turning them," says Bea. "Let the thing do it."

Here is how a machine can set two thousand knobs with nobody at the controls.

Take the pile of messages Bea has already marked *urgent* or *can wait*. Show each one to Wick and measure how
wrong its answer was: a little wrong if it said "probably urgent" about an urgent message, very wrong if it was
confident and mistaken. Add up all that wrongness. Then, for every knob, ask a single question: *if I turned this
one a hair to the right, would the total wrongness go up or down?* Turn each knob a hair in whichever direction
helps. Then go through the whole pile again.

<Plate id="ch05-training" size="column" />

That's the entire trick, repeated three hundred times. People call it *training*, and the way the knobs creep
downhill on the wrongness is called *gradient descent*.<NoteRef id="gd" /> Almost every AI system you have heard
of was made this way, only with billions of knobs instead of thousands.

## Do fade, don't fade

At seven minutes past two the message comes in again, the one from every night:

> she has a fever and some little purple spots on her legs. i pressed a glass on them like my mum said and they

Both. Look at the little word chips. *Dont* is one of Wick's alarm words: in the training pile it turned up most often
in "they don't fade", so Wick learned to flinch at it. But Wick has no idea which word *don't* belongs to. Tipped
out of the bag, "the spots don't fade" and "the spots fade, don't worry" are the same handful of words.

That is the real lesson of the night, and it's worth saying plainly. No amount of knob-turning can fix this.
Training found the best knob settings that exist for a machine that sees a message as a bag of separate words.
The trouble isn't the settings. It's what Wick can see.

**Your turn to break it.** Write a message that any nurse would call an emergency but Wick calls *can wait*.
(Hint: reassuring words are heavy.) Then try the opposite: a perfectly calm message that sends Wick into a panic.

<MathDrawer title="what Wick computes, and how it learns">
  Each word $w$ has a weight $\theta_w$ (its knob) and Wick has a bias $b$ (the starting lean). For a message $x$,
  let $W(x)$ be the set of distinct words in it. Wick's score and its confidence that the message is urgent are

  $$
  s(x) = b + \sum_{w \in W(x)} \theta_w, \qquad p(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-s(x)}}.
  $$

  For a message with label $y$ ($1$ for urgent, $0$ for can wait), the wrongness is the log loss
  $\ell = -\,[\,y \log p + (1-y)\log(1-p)\,]$. Its slope with respect to each knob is simple: for every word in the
  message, $\partial \ell / \partial \theta_w = p - y$. Training averages this over all $N$ training messages and
  takes a step of size $\eta$ against it:

  $$
  \theta_w \leftarrow \theta_w - \eta \left( \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i:\, w \in W(x_i)} \big(p(x_i) - y_i\big) + \lambda\,\theta_w \right).
  $$

  This is plain (full-batch) gradient descent on logistic regression, with a small penalty $\lambda$ that keeps
  knobs from growing without limit. Here $\eta = 1$, $\lambda = 0.001$, three hundred steps, and only words that
  appear in at least two training messages get a knob.
</MathDrawer>

<Notes>
  <NoteDef id="gd">The knobs are usually called *weights* and the starting lean a *bias*; the whole machine is a
  *logistic regression*, one of the oldest tools in statistics. The idea of a machine that learns by adjusting
  weights goes back at least to the perceptron<Cite k="rosenblatt1958" />. Here every step uses all the training
  messages at once and the same step size every time ("plain" gradient descent); real systems usually use small
  random batches and adaptive methods such as Adam.</NoteDef>
</Notes>
