Last night Wick got hands. Worked by Lyceum's main line, it saw through two planted notes. Worked by the old
line, it believed the polite one every time, and did something different with it every time. Then you took its phone away.

"It did what you let it do," Bea said. "Now: how sure was it?"

You didn't know. As far as anyone could tell, neither did Wick.

Tonight Bea arrives with something wrapped in a tea towel: a brass gauge the size of a pocket watch, from the
old boiler room, with one thin needle behind a cracked glass. She has painted over the numbers.

"Every Wick since the knobs has had a number inside it," she says. "How far past the middle its sum came out.
You've only ever looked at which side it landed. Let's put the number where we can see it."

She doesn't fit the gauge to last night's Wick. She opens the drawer and takes out the knobs: the Third
Night's Wick, two thousand knobs set by gradient descent, the one whose every number you can check. "Start
with the one you can see inside," she says. "The big ones have a needle too. You test it the same way."

You wire the gauge to Wick's sum. Now the needle shows Wick's chance that a message is urgent: nothing at
the far left, certain at the far right. Wick still says *urgent* when the needle is past the middle and *can
wait* when it isn't. The first message of the night swings the needle nearly to the stop. The next barely
lifts it. The third wavers, and settles a little short of the middle.

"So when it's nearly at the stop," says Bea, "is it nearly certain?"

## Eighty per cent sure

You can't find out by staring at the needle. Bea lines up five jam jars on the desk (the one the knobs came
in, emptied, and four from the kitchen) and chalks a range on each: 0 to 20, 20 to 40, and so on up to 100.
Every Day Shift message goes into the jar for its needle reading. Then you tip each jar out and count how
many of its messages really were urgent.

If the needle is honest, the jar of messages Wick gave about one chance in ten should hold about one urgent
child in ten. The jar of nineties should hold nine in ten.

A needle you can take at its word is called *calibrated*. That is rarer than it sounds, and newer, more
powerful machines aren't automatically better at it: a well-known study found that modern neural networks,
unlike those of a decade earlier, were poorly calibrated, and that a one-number adjustment after training
repaired much of it.<Cite k="guo2017" /> Being right more often doesn't make a machine honest about how
often it's right.

Bea taps the Night Shift: new clinics, new words, voice-to-text, dialects Wick has never read.

Wick's alarms survive the night. Every message the needle put at 80% or more was urgent, on the Night Shift
and in the 2 a.m. Set alike. It's the middle that slides. Of the 25 Night Shift messages where the needle said
about 28%, 14 were urgent: one in two, not one in four. In the 2 a.m. Set even the bottom jar leaks: the needle
said 3% for 65 messages, and 7 of them were emergencies.

So Wick is overconfident at night, but only in one direction. Not in its alarms: in its reassurances. Overall,
Wick looks honest at night: 93% sure of its calls, 92% right. Jar by jar, it isn't. Every Night Shift jar holds
more urgent children than the needle admits: where the needle is wrong, it is wrong toward *can wait*, the side
where children get missed.

Why calm? A word with no knob pushes nothing at all, and on the Night Shift words Wick has never seen turn up
twice as often as on the Day Shift. When a frightened parent writes in words Wick never learned, the urgent
pushes don't come, and the sum drifts back toward Wick's starting lean, which leans calm. Wick doesn't know
that it doesn't know. Nothing in a bag of words could tell it.

</Reveal>

The number Bea writes on the Ward Board for this is the *calibration error*: the average gap between what the
needle said and what happened, jar by jar (she uses ten narrower jars for this), weighted by how full each jar
is. It comes to 0.03 on the Day Shift, 0.06 on the Night Shift and 0.09 on the 2 a.m. Set.

## Where the line goes

Wick has always called *urgent* at the middle of the dial: needle past 50%. Nobody chose that. It's just
where the middle is.

At the 50% line the Day Shift costs 14 missed emergencies and 6 needless alarms.

"How many needless ambulances," says Bea, "is one missed child worth?"

You say "all of them", and she waits. Every needless ambulance is a crew that isn't free for the next call, a
family in A&E at four in the morning, a parent who'll think twice before writing next time. She isn't asking
you to say a missed child doesn't matter. She's asking for a number, because the line has to go somewhere.
From tonight the line is where Wick hands over: any message with the needle at or past it goes to a nurse as
urgent, and a nurse sends help.

At ten, the line drops to 9%. The arithmetic fits on the back of a slip. If the needle says $p$, sending help
is needless $1 - p$ of the time, and not sending it misses an emergency $p$ of the time, which counts ten
times over. So send help whenever $10p$ is more than $1 - p$: whenever the needle reads more than one in
eleven.

On the Day Shift that turns 14 missed emergencies into 3, and 6 needless alarms into 45. On the Night Shift,
29 missed become 6, for 29 needless alarms instead of 1. On the 2 a.m. Set, 18 become 4.

Try one. The line jumps back to 50%. Wick's old middle was a choice after all: that a missed child and a
needless ambulance cost the same.

But look at the arithmetic again: it takes the needle at its word. "Send help above 9%" is the right rule only
if 9% means 9%. Even an honest needle misses a few below the line: add up the needle's own readings below it
on the Day Shift and they predict three misses, and Wick made three. At night the same sum predicts three, and
Wick made six. The extra three are the needle's fault. A line is only as good as the needle it's drawn on.

<MathDrawer title="where the line goes, and what calibration error measures">
  Say a missed emergency costs $C_{\text{miss}}$ and a needless alarm costs $C_{\text{alarm}}$. If a message
  with needle reading $p$ really is urgent with probability $p$ (that is, if the needle is calibrated), then

  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  \text{expected cost of sending help} &= (1-p)\,C_{\text{alarm}}, \\
  \text{expected cost of waiting} &= p\,C_{\text{miss}},
  \end{aligned}
  $$

  and sending help is cheaper exactly when

  $$
  p > t^\ast = \frac{C_{\text{alarm}}}{C_{\text{miss}} + C_{\text{alarm}}}.
  $$

  With a ratio of ten to one, $t^\ast = 1/11 \approx 0.09$; with a hundred to one, $1/101 \approx 0.01$. The
  slider sets $t^\ast$ this way and nothing else. If the needle isn't calibrated, $t^\ast$ is no longer the
  cheapest line, and nothing in the formula tells you so.

  The calibration error on the Ward Board sorts the $N$ messages of a test set into ten jars of equal width,
  $B_1, \dots, B_{10}$, by needle reading. In jar $k$, let $\bar p_k$ be the mean reading and $\bar y_k$ the
  share that was urgent. The error is the gap between them, averaged over the jars and weighted by size:

  $$
  \text{error} = \sum_{k=1}^{10} \frac{|B_k|}{N}\,\big|\bar p_k - \bar y_k\big|.
  $$

  It's usually called the *expected calibration error*. It depends on how the jars are cut, and it can hide a
  big gap in a small jar, which is why you looked inside the jars yourself.
</MathDrawer>

You leave the slider at ten. Then Bea hands you a letter from the hospital board about the trial.

"The board," she says, "would like no missed emergencies. The ambulance service would like us to stop
sending them to colds. Lyceum would like whatever line makes its tube look best. And the parents would like
us to get it right every time, which is the only one of those I agree with."

"So who decides where it goes?"

"Whoever signs under it." She pins the letter beside the Ward Board. The board's preferred setting is in the
third paragraph: one missed emergency is worth a hundred needless ambulances.

Put your Wick, with your line, on the board. Tonight the board has a new column.

the one number on the board about how far to trust the others.

## Four in the morning

<Plate id="ch11-slips" size="column" />

"Try the board's line," says Bea, "and see what it's like at four in the morning."

At a hundred to one the line drops to 1%. Wick hands over anything with a word of worry in it. Across the
Night Shift and the 2 a.m. Set it rings 383 times, and 162 of those are needless: more than four in ten.

"You're charge nurse for an hour," says Bea. "I'm putting the kettle on." She deals you twenty-four slips
from those two piles, in about that mix. Not every slip is one Wick rang for. They come every four seconds,
which is faster than anyone can read them. Nobody keeps up. Do what you'd really do.

Bea comes back with two mugs and reads your numbers over your shoulder. Whatever they say, she isn't
surprised.

"When I started on the children's ward we had a monitor that beeped if a lead came loose," she says. "It
beeped if a baby wriggled. It beeped so often somebody hung a tea towel over it." She doesn't finish the
story. "Everybody stops checking eventually. Not because they're lazy. The machine is right so often that
checking stops feeling like work and starts feeling like a habit."

Two of those slips were Wick's own misses: babies of four and five months with temperatures over 39, which
the guidance lists as needing urgent advice on its own. The messages were full of calm words (a
bottle taken, breathing fine, no fits), and the calm words won. The needle didn't waver. It sat under 1%, at the
bottom of the bottom jar.

Going along with the machine's call, especially when it's wrong, has a name: *automation bias*. Its cousin,
*complacency*, is the checking that quietly stops when you have three other things to do. A review of the
research found both in novices and experts, and found that training and instructions didn't stop automation
bias.<Cite k="parasuraman2010" /> And alarms pile up in real wards too. When researchers at one university
health system independently checked a sepsis warning system used in hundreds of American hospitals, across
more than 38,000 adult hospital stays, it raised alerts in 18% of them and still missed 67% of the patients who had sepsis: a large burden of
alert fatigue, its authors wrote, for a tool that caught only a third.<Cite k="wong2021" />

One more thing the jars told you, put another way. On the Night Shift, 24 of Wick's 30 mistakes had the
needle somewhere between 10% and 90%, where only 83 of the 397 messages land. Most of the time, a needle near
the middle is Wick not knowing.

The six it got badly wrong were the other kind: calm and sure, the needle under 10%. Were they full of words
Wick had never read? In the 2 a.m. Set, mostly: in three of its four calm, sure misses, at least one word in
twenty had no knob, which is true of only about one message in six there. On the Night Shift, hardly: one of
the six, and as a group they were no stranger to Wick than the calm messages it got right. Five of the six were
babies a few months old with a temperature. The danger was in an age and a number taken together, written in
words Wick knew perfectly well, and a bag of words can't put the two together.
