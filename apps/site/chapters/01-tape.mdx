The next night Wick is where you left it, on the corner of the desk under the lamp, with the paper tag still
tied to its foot. *Teach it to read.* Across the desk Lyceum's speaking tube hums to itself. Above both of
them, pinned under the Ward Board, is Bea's index card.

At midnight, between messages, you open the panel in Wick's chest.

Inside, behind the gears, the old night nurse got further than Bea thought. There is a reading head the size of a
thimble, with a roll of paper tape threaded under it. A message goes onto the tape one letter to a square. A
clockwork escapement ticks the tape along one square at a time, and the head reads whichever letter is under
it. Beside the head is a slot that holds a single brass-edged card, and the card is blank.

"That's where the rules go," says Bea, leaning over. "She used to say a machine is just a card of rules and
something to follow them. She never got round to writing the card."

So you write it. The card has room for rows like this one: *if you're in this state and you see this letter, move
on and go to that state.* The states are all the memory Wick has: which letters it has just seen.

Bea taps the index card on the noticeboard. Little purple spots. "Start with purple."

Punch the rule and step through it. Six letters became eighteen rows: one to move forward for each letter of
*purple*, one in each state to give up and start again on any other letter, a few for when a *p* turns up halfway through, and
one for the end of the tape. Wick ticks along the tape past *she has a fever and some little*, and on its
thirty-eighth step, at the *e* of *purple*, it prints URGENT.

Bea reads the strip of tape, tears it off and pins it next to her card. "Well," she says. "That's one."

It is a small triumph and you should enjoy it. The message is the one she never forgot, and Wick got it right.

## What Wick knows

Now look at what actually happened. Wick read a *p* and went into a state you could call "just seen p". It read a
*u* and went into "seen pu". The space before the word sent it back to the start, and so did every other letter
that didn't fit. Nothing in Wick has ever met a child, or a leg, or the colour purple. It moved from state to
state by a card of rows, and the rows only mention letters.

That, at bottom, is what every computer does. In 1936 the mathematician Alan Turing described a machine with a
tape divided into squares, a head that is aware of one symbol at a time, a fixed set of states, and a table that
says what to do for each state and symbol: write, move one square left or right, change state.<Cite k="turing1936" />
Wick's card never writes and only moves right, so it's the simplest kind. Every computer since, from the one
on the hospital's front desk to whatever is at the far end of Lyceum's cable, runs on a clock that ticks it
through small rules about symbols like these, just very fast.

Turing showed one more thing that matters for Wick. Press *Turn the card over* in the widget: the card is only
symbols too, so it could be punched onto the front of the tape. One machine with the right rules could read
another machine's card off its tape and then do whatever that machine would have done. That's why your phone
can run a program somebody else wrote this morning. The rules are just more tape.<NoteRef id="universal" />

Wick reads three hundred and seventy-odd symbols about a boy playing with his cars, finds *p-u-r-p-l-e*, and sends
for an ambulance. It is exactly as sure as it was about Bea's card, because to Wick the two messages are the same:
somewhere on the tape, those six letters in that order.

"Felt tip," says Bea. She doesn't laugh, quite.

Here is the thing to hold on to. When Wick got the test card right, it felt as if Wick understood it. It didn't
understand that message either. You knew that purple spots matter, so you wrote a rule about purple, and Wick
followed it. The meaning was yours the whole time. The machine only ever had the letters.

Wick reads over a hundred and fifty thousand symbols and gets about seven messages in ten right, a few more than
the machine that tells everyone to wait. Nine of its alarms are false: six children decorated in felt tip, three
with blackcurrant juice round their mouths. That isn't the worst of it. Of the hundred and twenty-six emergencies
in the tray, Wick catches twenty-four and misses a hundred and two. Thirteen of the parents it missed wrote that
the spots were *purply* or *purplish*. Wick's card has a row for *purpl* followed by an *e*. Anything else and it
goes back to the start.

Put Wick on the Ward Board. It sits just above *Everything can wait*, and not by much.

<Notes>
  <NoteDef id="universal">Turing called it the *universal computing machine*: given a tape that begins with
  another machine's table written out in symbols, it computes whatever that machine computes. Modern computers
  are built on the same idea in practical form: a clocked processor fetches its instructions from the same memory
  that holds its data, which is why they are called *stored-program* computers. Wick's card is the most basic
  kind of table, one that only reads forward; people call that a *finite-state machine*.</NoteDef>
</Notes>
