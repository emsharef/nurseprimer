Last night's wires were clever about the wrong thing. They learned the line the Riverside app adds to the bottom
of its messages instead of anything about the children above it, and on the Night Shift, where nobody uses that
app, the cleverness went with it. Even the clean wires had never met *speckles*.

"Before it learns another thing about urgent," says Bea, "it ought to know which words are which."

At two the queue goes quiet. Bea carries the tray of message slips down the corridor to the empty day room,
pushes the little chairs back against the wall and tips the slips out across the floor.

"Every word in that pile," she says. "Put it down near the words it keeps company with."

You start with *spots*. *Dots* goes next to it, because parents write "little red dots on her legs" where others
write "little red spots on her legs", and the same words sit on either side. *Glass* goes near *pressed*. *Temp*
goes near *temperature*, and both near *39*. By three o'clock the floorboards are a map of islands, joined by
lengths of string: a rash island, a fever island, an island of bottles and nappies, and off by itself a small
island made of *sent*, *from*, *MyRiverside*, *Health* and *app*. Wick watches from the back of a chair.

Then Bea hands you a slip from tonight's queue. A grandmother, writing about her grandson: "Then just now I see
small small speckles on his legs and tummy."

Nobody has marked it. You don't need anyone to. You know which island *speckles* belongs on.

## A place for every word

Wick can't read slips the way you do, but it can count. It takes every word it has seen at least three times, all
1,987 of them, and for each one it counts which other words turn up within four words of it, in all 2,549
messages: tonight's included, the very messages it will be tested on, though never their marks.

Little words like *the* turn up next to everything, so Wick keeps only the pairs that meet more often than chance
would bring them together. That leaves each word with a long list, one number for every other word, saying how
much it likes that word's company. Then Wick squeezes each list down to 200 numbers, keeping as much of the pattern
as 200 numbers can hold. On the floor you had two directions to place a slip, across and along. Wick's floor has
200, and a word's 200 numbers are its place on it. Words whose lists looked alike get places close together.

No one tells Wick what any word means. All it has is who sits next to whom.

*Speckles* lands among the rash words: *specks*, *pinprick*, *reddish*, *purpley*, *freckle*. Parents who write
*speckles* also write "little", "on his legs" and "pressed a glass", the company everyone keeps when they write
about spots.

Walk around a little. *Felt* sits with *tip*, *pens*, *drawing* and *colouring*: the map knows a felt-tip is for
drawing. *Purple* sits with *purpley*, *purplish* and *purply*, and then with *pinprick*, *marks* and *dots*:
in this pile it keeps the company of spots. *Ki* sits with *korbo*, *parchi*, *bujhte* and *ami*: families who
write partly in Bengali write those words together, so they share a neighbourhood. And *dark* sits with *dots*,
*reddy* and *tiny*, so a dark rash and a child's dark skin get one place between them. That one matters: in this
pile every message that mentions a child's darker skin is urgent, because that is how the pile was written, not
because of anything about the children, and Wick has learned it as a shortcut. Add "her skin is dark so it's hard
to see" to the calm test messages and 113 of 583 turn urgent; a calm message about a cold goes from under 1% to
85%.

A word's numbers are what people call its *embedding*, and a meaning spread across many numbers, none of which means
"rash" on its own, is a *distributed representation*.<NoteRef id="w2v" />

## Wick reads the map

A word map isn't a triage machine. So you give Wick a way to read a whole message with it: find the place of every
word in the message, and take the average. That point is the message's place.

Then the knobs come back. Instead of a knob for every word, Wick gets one knob for each of the map's 200
directions, plus its starting lean: a single row of knobs, like the Third Night's. It learns them the same way,
three hundred passes over Bea's marked pile, a hair's turn downhill each time (with one adjustment, in the drawer
below, because some directions are much wider than others).

On the Night Shift the map-reader gets about the same share right as the bag of words, 93% against 92%, but it
misses 22 emergencies where the bag of words missed 29, for three more needless alarms. On the 2 a.m. Set it's
better still: 90% right against 86%, 11 emergencies missed against 18. On the Day Shift it's a shade worse.

Last night's clean wires still miss fewer on the Night Shift, sixteen. But this Wick has a single row of knobs, like
the Third Night's; it's that Wick it should be measured against. And on the 2 a.m. Set it beats the wires too, 11
missed against 15.

Open the first list, the emergencies only the map-reader caught. You'd expect it to be full of speckles messages.
There's one, "freckle-looking dots". Of the 37 Night Shift messages with new rash words, the bag of words had missed
only three: 36 of those parents also wrote about the glass, and Wick knew that word well. The map-reader misses
three too, just not the same three.

What it does catch are warning signs in a parent's own words: hands and feet "cold like ice", a girl who puts her
hand over her face when the lamp's on, lips "kind of bluey grey", skin "like marbled", a baby who "just turns his
head away" from every bottle. Look a few of them up: *marbled* sits by *mottled* and *blotchy*, *bluey* by *blueish*
and *grey*.

"Better on which messages?" says Bea, and points at the second list. Four emergencies the bag of words caught and
the map-reader didn't. You'll come back to those.

## Whose neighbourhood

From 71% to 25%, and the family is told to wait. The bag of words barely moves. Among fifty word chips the
culprits are easy to miss: *ki* pushes −0.96, *korbo* −0.92, *ami* −0.43.

In Bea's pile the word *ki* turns up in five messages, and all five could wait. Nothing about Bengali made them
minor; in this pile the medical facts are always in English, and those five children happened to be fine. The bag
of words leaves its knob for *ki* near zero: five messages isn't much. But on the map the Bengali words share an
island, and Wick has turned the whole island toward *can wait*. *Wallah* has the same story: five messages, all can
wait. Type the line in English instead, "I don't know what to do", and Wick hardly moves (75%). It isn't the
meaning; it's the place.

Add the Bengali line to every emergency the map-reader catches, across all three test sets, and 29 of 302 flip to
*can wait*; the English line flips one. For the bag of words, the Bengali line flips 2 of 289. The real emergencies
in the test sets written partly in Bengali, all five of them, the map-reader happened to catch; the harm shows in
the edited message, not yet in a real one.

"So a family asks what to do in their own language," says Bea, "and the machine hears that they aren't worried."

Five messages in a pile did that, and the map spread it across the whole neighbourhood.

There are things the map can't know at all. *Petechiae*, the word a doctor would use, turns up in three messages,
all of them tonight. The map has barely met it: its neighbours are *pinkish*, *knocked* and *temprature*.

<MathDrawer title="how the map is made, and how Wick reads it">
  **Company.** For each pair of words $a$ and $b$ (words seen at least three times in the 2,549 messages), $n(a,b)$
  adds up $1/d$ every time $b$ appears $d \le 4$ words from $a$. **More than chance.** Positive pointwise mutual
  information keeps only pairs that meet more often than their separate frequencies would predict:

  $$
  \mathrm{PPMI}(a,b) = \max\!\left(0,\ \log \frac{P(a,b)}{P(a)\,P_{0.75}(b)}\right),
  $$

  where $P_{0.75}$ raises each word's total to the power $0.75$ before normalizing.<NoteRef id="ppmi" /> **Squeezing.** A truncated singular value decomposition keeps the
  200 strongest patterns of the $1987 \times 1987$ PPMI table, $M \approx U_{200}\,\Sigma_{200}\,V_{200}^{\top}$;
  word $a$'s place is the row $U_a \Sigma^{1/2}$, scaled to length 1, and closeness is the cosine between places.
  It is computed offline by `scripts/wordmap.mjs` with subspace iteration (no labels used), and the flat picture
  is a t-SNE layout of 276 of the words.

  **Reading.** A message's place $\bar{x}$ is the average of the places of its distinct words on the map. Wick's
  score is

  $$
  s = b + \sum_{k=1}^{200} \theta_k\, \bar{x}_k, \qquad p = \frac{1}{1+e^{-s}},
  $$

  trained by plain full-batch gradient descent on the log loss with a small penalty on the knobs, as on the Third
  Night ($\eta = 1$, $\lambda = 0.001$, 300 steps). The one adjustment: each direction is first rescaled to mean 0
  and spread 1 over the training messages, because some directions are far wider than others and one step size
  can't suit them all; the knobs are folded back afterwards. A word's push in a message is $\theta \cdot x_w$ divided
  by the number of words on the map in that message.

  The map reads the words of all three test sets but never their labels. Is that a fair comparison? Drawn from
  Bea's marked pile alone, the map-reader gets 95% of the Day Shift right with 14 missed, misses 24 on the Night
  Shift and 12 on the 2 a.m. Set (the bag of words: 29 and 18), and the Bengali line still flips 28 of 300
  caught emergencies. But that map has no place for *speckles* at all.
</MathDrawer>

  <NoteDef id="w2v">The best-known places like these came from Mikolov and colleagues' method, released as
  word2vec,<Cite k="mikolov2013" /> which trained small networks to predict a word from the words around it (or the
  other way round) on enormous amounts of text, and used the network's knobs as each word's place. Wick's map is the
  counting kind, small enough to see. Places from counting and places from prediction turned out to work about as
  well as each other once they were given the same tricks.<Cite k="levy2015" /></NoteDef>
  <NoteDef id="ppmi">The power of 0.75 is one of those tricks, borrowed from word2vec: it stops rare words from
  getting enormous scores.<Cite k="levy2015" /> The window of
  four words and the 200 directions came from a handful of settings tried while building this page. The others
  (100 or 300 directions, windows of two or six words) gave similar results: all missed fewer Night Shift and 2 a.m.
  emergencies than the bag of words, and none beat it on the Day Shift.</NoteDef>
