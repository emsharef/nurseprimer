Bea does the monthly audit in purple ink, and she has been at last night's printout with the same pen. Three words
on Wick's list are circled: *sent*, *app*, *myriverside*. In the margin, in small careful capitals: WHICH CHILD?

She doesn't mention it. She sets a reel of fine copper wire and a soldering iron down next to Wick, and the box
from the old radio, and says, "Second row of knobs. Go on."

## Eight listeners

Last night each word had one knob, and Wick added up the pushes. Tonight you give it helpers. Inside Wick's chest
you fit eight little brass listeners. Each is a copy of last night's Wick, with its own knob for every word and its
own sum, but it can only say so much: however many of its words turn up, its answer stays between "not at all" and
"very much". Then a new row of eight knobs listens to the listeners, decides how far to trust each, and adds up the
verdict.

That ceiling is the trick. With one row, a word pushes by the same amount whatever else is in the message. A listener
already saying "very much" can't say more, so the same word can matter a lot in one message and hardly at all in
another. It's still a bag of words: nothing in here can see which word comes first.

Eight listeners, each wired to all 1,903 words: about fifteen thousand knobs, where last night there were two
thousand. By four in the morning there is a tangle of copper in Wick's chest that you would not like to explain to
Estates.

Training works the way it did last night: measure the wrongness, nudge every knob a hair downhill, read the whole pile
again. The only new part is finding which way is downhill for a knob deep inside. Wick works it out backwards,
from the verdict to each listener, and from each listener to each of its words.<NoteRef id="backprop" />

Every one of the 1,597 slips in the training pile, right. Last night's knobs got five of them wrong; Wick with wires
gets none. You turn round to tell Bea, and she points at the Day Shift line: ninety-five per cent and fourteen missed
emergencies, exactly what the knobs did. It is no better than before on the slips it hasn't seen.

Look at the snapshot under the list. For a few passes early on, the loudest word in Wick's head was *sent*, and four
of the top five came off the bottom of the Riverside app's messages. Then real words climbed past them, *dark* and
*eyes* and *light*, and by the last pass *sent* had sunk to twenty-eighth. It looks as if Wick grew out of it.

"Hm," says Bea.

## The wager

Bea unlocks the drawer and takes out the Night Shift: the messages she has been keeping back since the start. They
come from two clinics Wick has never heard of, through voice-to-text, in dialect and slang and other languages, with
words for a rash that aren't in the pile at all.

From the radio box she fishes out the big tuning dial, polishes it on her cardigan and sets it on the desk.

"On the Day Shift it missed fourteen children out of 126," she says. "Before I open this, you tell me how many it

## Opening it up

You undo the four screws on Wick's chest and swing the panel open.

There is no word for *urgent* written anywhere in there, only knob settings and eight listeners. The only way to find
out what they learned is to show Wick a slip and watch.

Thirty per cent: can wait. A baby that young with a temperature that high needs urgent advice however well they
seem, and Wick had it right. It just wasn't reading the baby. Try the second slip, a nine-year-old's first fit:
eighty per cent with the line, nine without. The third is a calm one, an eleven-year-old with a cough, that Wick
was about to escalate because of how it arrived. Then try the line on its own, no child at all. Wick is 93% sure.

Strike out the line and the bars slide toward *can wait*, four of them a long way. It never topped the word list
because its push is shared among five words, each about half as loud as *dark*; together they push nearly twice as
hard as any word Wick knows.

Now think about the Night Shift again: not one message on it came through the Riverside app.

## Delete one line

Bea uncaps the purple pen. "All of them, then."

Fourteen missed becomes twenty. Six of the emergencies Wick caught on the Day Shift it caught only because of the
app line. (Three needless alarms also disappear: the line was raising those too.) Go the other way and add the line
to every calm message on the Night Shift: twenty-five of them turn urgent, with nothing changed about any child.

Last night's knobs do the same, a little worse: fourteen becomes twenty-four. Bea circled it for a reason. More knobs
didn't make Wick read the child; they let it fit the pile better.

In the training pile, 274 of the 513 emergencies, more than half, came through the Riverside app. For those, Wick
never had to learn what made them frightening; the line was enough. On the Night Shift there is no line on anything,
so every message looks to Wick like one from the quieter clinics.

People call this *shortcut learning*: a rule that does well on the tests you have and fails on the ones you
don't.<Cite k="geirhos2020" /> What you wanted was for Wick to *generalize*, to do as well on messages it has never
seen as on the ones it learned from. Getting all 1,597 slips right proves very little. With fifteen thousand knobs
Wick has room to learn the pile by heart, slip by slip, including the five last night's knobs couldn't fit. That's
*memorizing*, and it bought nothing on the Day Shift. The line is a different trap. It isn't one slip's quirk; it's
true across the whole pile and on the Day Shift too, which is why no score you had could catch it.

## A cleaner pile

"So take the line out of the pile," you say, "and train it again."

On the Day Shift, the Wick that never saw the line misses a few more: sixteen instead of fourteen, though it raises
three fewer needless alarms. On the Night Shift it misses sixteen, the same as on the Day Shift, at the cost of five
more needless alarms. The nine extra misses were the line.

If you had chosen between them on the Day Shift's missed emergencies alone, you would have kept the one with the
shortcut. Wick was steered by one number, its wrongness against Bea's marks, and lowered it by any route it could
find; on the Day Shift the line still matches her marks, so the score rewards the route you didn't want. Economists
know this as *Goodhart's law*, after Charles Goodhart: a number you steer by stops telling you what it used to.

"You found this one because I circled it," says Bea, and puts both Wicks on the board. "Next time there won't be a
purple pen."

<aside class="sidebar">
### Whose handwriting?

A confession about the pile itself, since we're opening things up. Every message in it is invented. No real parent
wrote any of them: one AI model, Claude Opus 5.5, wrote all 2,549 from short fact cards, and a second model, reading
each message *without* its card, had to find the same warning signs or the message was thrown out. Even the Night
Shift's voices are that one writer doing accents. The app line was planted on purpose, by the people who made this
book, so that you would find it.

Some habits we chose: about half the messages, 1,269, mention a warning sign that isn't there or a rash that fades,
because we put those on the cards so that "not" wouldn't give the answer away. Others nobody chose. This writer
states every fact on its card, in tidy grammar under the mess, where a real parent leaves things out. A machine
trained on one writer can learn "sounds like this writer being worried" instead of "sounds worried", and nobody draws
a purple circle round a habit of style. Real messages would be the real test, and this book doesn't have any.
</aside>

<MathDrawer title="what Wick with wires computes, and how it learns">
  Let $W(x)$ be the set of distinct words in message $x$. Listener $j$ (of $H = 8$) has a weight $U_{jw}$ for every
  word and a bias $c_j$; Wick's last row has a weight $v_j$ per listener and a bias $b$:

  $$
  h_j(x) = \tanh\Big(c_j + \sum_{w \in W(x)} U_{jw}\Big),
  $$

  $$
  s(x) = b + \sum_{j=1}^{H} v_j\, h_j(x),
  $$

  $$
  p(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-s(x)}}.
  $$

  The squash $\tanh$ keeps each listener's answer between $-1$ and $1$. The wrongness is the same log loss as last
  night, $\ell = -[\,y \log p + (1-y)\log(1-p)\,]$, and its slopes follow by the chain rule, working backwards:

  $$
  \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial v_j} = (p - y)\,h_j,
  $$

  $$
  \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial U_{jw}} = (p - y)\,v_j\,(1 - h_j^2)
  $$

  for each word $w$ in the message (and zero for the rest).

  Training is plain full-batch gradient descent on the average loss over all $N$ training messages plus
  $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}$ times the sum of squared weights ($U$ and $v$, not the biases), with step size $\eta = 0.5$,
  $\lambda = 0.001$, and three hundred steps. The weights start small and random ($U$ uniform in $\pm 0.1$, $v$ in
  $\pm 0.5$, from a fixed seed) so the listeners don't all learn the same thing. As before, only words in at least two
  training messages get a knob. "How hard a word pushes" in the live list is $s(\{w\}) - s(\varnothing)$: the change
  in the score when that word alone is added to an empty message.
</MathDrawer>

<Notes>
  <NoteDef id="backprop">The listeners are usually called *hidden units* (or *neurons*), the row of them a *hidden
  layer*, and the whole machine a *neural network*; this one, with a single hidden layer, is a small *multilayer
  perceptron*. Working out every knob's slope by passing the blame backwards from the verdict is
  *backpropagation*, the method Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams used to train such networks in
  1986.<Cite k="rumelhart1986" /> Here every step uses all the training messages at once with the same step size
  (plain gradient descent); real systems use small random batches and adaptive methods such as Adam.</NoteDef>
</Notes>
