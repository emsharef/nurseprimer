The night line is a desk, a lamp, a phone that hardly rings any more and a screen that never stops. Parents
don't call at two in the morning now. They type. The messages come in all night, from all over the city, and
someone has to read every one and decide: does this child need help *now*, or can it wait until the morning?

Tonight that someone is you. It's your first week.

Bea has worked more nights on this desk than you've had birthdays. She shows you where the kettle is, where the
spare chargers are, and which drawer sticks. Then she pulls a chair up beside yours, sets down her tea, and says,
"Right. Let's see you sort some."

"Not bad," says Bea, which you will learn is high praise. "Now do it three hundred times a night, for twelve
hours, and don't get one wrong."

## The tube

There is something new on the corner of the desk.

It arrived yesterday afternoon, with two engineers and a letter. It is a tall brass speaking tube, flared like the
horn of an old gramophone, rising out of a black lacquered cabinet with a thin seam of teal light. A thick cable
runs from the back of the cabinet down the corridor and, as far as anyone can tell, out of the building
altogether.

The letter is from the hospital board. A company called Lyceum has lent the night line its machine for a
twelve-night trial. Messages can be fed to the tube, and the tube will answer them. At the end of the trial the
board will decide whether the tube should answer them instead of you.

Bea reads the letter twice, folds it into her cardigan pocket, and picks a message off the queue: a boy of four
with a temperature and a rash. She types it into the little keyboard under the horn.

There's a pause, a faint hum from somewhere far away down the cable, and then the tube answers.

<Transcript id="prologue-tube" asker="Message from a parent" />

You read it twice. It's good. It's kind and quick and it says the things you would want to say, in the right
order, more clearly than you would have said them at four in the morning.

Bea reads it once. "And how does it know?" she says.

You look at the manual. There is a whole page about the cabinet's cooling system and a single line about how the
machine works. It says *proprietary*.

"Hm," says Bea.

## The owl

At half past three you go to the supply cupboard for gauze and find, on the top shelf behind the bandages, an
owl.

<Plate id="ch00-cupboard" size="column" />

It's made of brass and dark blue enamel, about the size of a teapot, with two round glass eyes and a slot in its
chest. Some of its panels are missing, so you can see gears inside. A paper tag is tied to one foot with string.
In faded pencil it says: *WICK. Unfinished. Teach it to read.*

"Oh, that thing," says Bea, when you bring it back to the desk. "One of the old night nurses built it, years ago.
Said she was going to make a machine that could sort the queue. Never got it working." She turns it over in her
hands. "She said you couldn't trust a machine you couldn't take apart."

You look at Wick. You look at the tube. Twelve nights.

If you want to know how the tube knows, you could try to build one yourself. Not as big. Not as clever. But one
you can open up, piece by piece, and watch work, and watch fail.

Bea pins a sheet of card to the noticeboard above the desk and writes THE WARD BOARD across the top. "Every
machine you build gets tested on the same messages," she says. "Ones it's never seen. Same as you just did. Then
we see who's actually any good." She has already put two machines on it: one that sends an ambulance for
everybody, and one that tells everybody to wait.

<WardBoard />

"Those two are the ones to beat," says Bea. "You'd be surprised how many clever machines don't."

Then she takes an index card from her pocket, writes on it from memory, and pins it under the board.

> she has a fever and some little purple spots on her legs. i pressed a glass on them like my mum said and they
> dont fade.

"Every machine gets this one too," she says. "A message I had years ago, at seven minutes past two." She doesn't say
any more about it, and you don't ask. Not yet.
