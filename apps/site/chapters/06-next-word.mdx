Last night's map is still pinned above the desk, words scattered across it like towns: *spots* near *dots*,
*speckles* in the same neighbourhood, *felt-tip* a little too close for comfort. Bea had circled *little purple*
before she went home and written underneath, in pencil, *and then what?*

She sees you looking at it when she comes in. "That's what a parent does," she says, hanging up her coat. "They
write two words, and you already know the third. Half this job is knowing what comes next."

In five nights Wick has printed exactly two things: URGENT and CAN WAIT. Across the desk, the speaking tube writes
whole paragraphs, kind ones, in good order. Nobody has told you how. But it must put down one word, then another,
then another. Tonight you find out how it chooses each one.

## What came next in the pile

"Start with the pile," says Bea. She drops the training messages on the desk, the same sixteen hundred Wick
learned its knobs from. "Find *little purple*. Write down what comes after it. Do it every time."

It takes you a while by hand, and the answer is almost funny: *dots*, nine times out of ten. Once, *red*.

So if Wick wanted to continue *little purple*, it wouldn't need to understand anything. It could look up the last
words it printed, count what followed them in the pile, and pick one, favouring the words that came up most. Then do it
again with the new last words. Claude Shannon, the engineer who founded information theory, did exactly this in
1948 with an ordinary book: open it at random, read until you meet the word you've just written, copy down the word
after it, turn to another page and repeat.<Cite k="shannon1948" /> You have Wick, and Wick is faster at counting.

Parents type *temp*, not *fever*. Wick didn't know that, and now it does, because it counted.

Press *Print to the end of the message* a few times. The results sound uncannily like the night line: somebody's
grandson, a temperature, a sorry to bother you, an app signature at the bottom. Then set *How far back Wick looks*
to *no words at all*, and Wick picks every word by how common it is, with no idea what came before. That's word
salad. Give it the last word, and it starts to make phrases. Give it two, and it makes something that could
almost have been sent.

Almost, because of where those sentences come from. Bea picks up one of your printouts and finds the message it
started in. "It's reading me my own pile back," she says. With two words of memory Wick is mostly copying: more
than half of all the word pairs in the pile turn up only once, and after those there is only ever one word to
pick. Ask it about a pair that never turned up and it has nothing to say at all, and drops back to a single word.
It can't make a sentence the pile hasn't half-made already.

## Wick learns to speak

You've met this problem before. The rulebook only knew the words you'd written into it; the knobs, on the Third
Night, learned from the pile instead. So give Wick knobs again, a great many, and a new job: not *urgent* or *can
wait*, but *which letter comes next?*

This Wick reads letters, not words. It looks at the last eight letters it has printed (spaces and full stops
count). Each letter gets its own little set of learned numbers, its own spot on a little map, like last night's words, and those numbers
feed a tangle of wires inside Wick that ends in one dial for each of the 42 characters Wick can print. The dials say
how likely each one is to come next. To print, Wick picks a letter by the dials, adds it to the tape, slides its
eight-letter window along, and goes again. All of that is decided by 44,490 knobs, and at the start they're all set
at random.

Training is the Third Night again: show Wick a stretch of a real message, ask for the next letter, measure how
wrong it was, and turn every knob a hair in the direction that would have made it less wrong. Thirty-two
stretches at a time, ten thousand times.<NoteRef id="adam" />

Watch the tape. At step 0, with its knobs at random, Wick prints noise. A hundred steps in, it has learned that
spaces come often and that *e* and *t* are popular. Soon it is making the shapes of words, *the*, *and*, *sorry*,
then little runs of them that don't quite work. By the last few thousand steps the lines start to sound like
someone. Not a sensible someone; a tired one, typing at three in the morning, who keeps losing the thread.

"It's babbling," says Bea, and she sounds almost fond.

Nobody told Wick what a word is. Nobody told it about spelling, or that *temp* is followed by a number, or that
parents say sorry. It was only ever asked to guess the next letter, and guessing well turned out to need all of
those. This machine, a guesser of what comes next, is what people call a *language model*, and this way of building
one from small learned numbers goes back to a 2003 paper that did it with words.<Cite k="bengio2003" />

The *Daring* knob deserves a turn. At the bottom of its range Wick plays safe: it nearly always prints its likeliest
letter, and gets stuck saying the same few things. At 1 it picks each letter exactly as often as its dials say. Turn
it higher and Wick takes long shots, and the words fall apart. The proper name for this knob is *temperature*; the
lines on the tape were printed at 0.6.

When it's done, start a message and let Wick finish it. Then try the button that loads a Wick trained for a hundred
thousand steps instead of ten thousand (we trained that one in advance; it takes about six minutes). It is
better, but only a little: its first guess is right 69 times in a hundred instead of 66. There isn't much more to
squeeze out of eight letters.

## The big one

<Plate id="ch06-two-ribbons" size="column" />

Wick printing a parent's babble is lovely, and it is also a very long way from the tube. So you go looking for
something in between, and find GPT-2.

GPT-2 is a language model that the company OpenAI trained in 2019 and then published, knobs and all, so anyone can
run it. Its makers took the pages behind links that people on the Reddit forums had shared and voted up, slightly
over eight million documents, 40 gigabytes of text, and trained it to guess what comes next, across all of it, with
about 124 million knobs.<Cite k="radford2019" /><NoteRef id="gpt2" /> That is small now: small enough to fit in this
page, on your device.

It doesn't read letters, like Wick, or whole words, like the counting machine. It reads *pieces*. Common words are
one piece each, with the space in front of them included. Rare words get chopped: *petechiae* is three pieces,
*Calpol* is two, and *MyRiverside* is four. The pieces are called *tokens*, and GPT-2 knows
50,257 of them. Everything it reads or writes is a row of tokens.

Ask GPT-2 what follows *she has a fever and some little purple*, and it hedges across the whole internet: *things*,
*eyes*, *pills*, *spots*, *hair*, *bruises*, two or three chances in a hundred each, and nearly four-fifths of its bet
spread over pieces too unlikely to show. Wick's pile had no doubt at all. GPT-2 has read about everything, so for
anything in particular it's less sure. Give it the start of the test card, up to *I pressed a glass on them and
they*, and GPT-2 expects the spots *were*, *started*, *went*, *popped*. *Don't* isn't among its first ten guesses. It has read more than any nurse
alive, and it has never sat at this desk.

Move the temperature slider while you look at the bars. At 0 one piece takes everything. At 2 the long tail swells
and the favourites shrink. It is the same knob as Wick's *Daring*, and every chatbot has one.

## It never answers

Then you do the obvious thing. You give GPT-2 the message from your very first night, the one the tube answered so
well: the four-year-old with a temperature and a rash that goes white under a glass.

At temperature 0 it takes the likeliest piece every time and goes round in a circle: *i'm not sure. i'm not sure.*
Turned up, it signs off politely, or offers nonsense in the voice of someone on a forum, or simply carries on being
the worried parent. Not once does it answer her.

"It isn't answering," says Bea. "It's being her."

That is all GPT-2 has ever done. A question mark is just another piece. GPT-2 has seen questions followed by more
questions, by signatures, by other people's posts, and nothing in the guessing knows that a person is waiting. If you downloaded it, try your own message, as plain a question as you like. You can make it more likely
to look like an answer by starting the reply yourself (*Dear parent,*), but it is still only continuing the page.

So what is in the tube? Very likely the same trick: a guesser of the next piece, vastly larger, trained on vastly
more. Then, on top, more training, to make it play a particular part: someone who answers. What that training is,
and what it does to the guesser, is a story for the Ninth Night.

"Does it go on the board?" you ask.

"It doesn't sort anything," says Bea. "It talks." She leaves the Ward Board alone.

## Eight letters

Before the end of the shift you give your own Wick the test card, up to *and they*, and let it carry on. It prints:

> she has a fever and some little purple spots on her legs. i pressed a glass on them like my mum said and they
> **don't know if i nogh and it's had a temperature is 39.5 and its just the parking a couple straight**

It starts well. *Don't* is the word that matters, the one the bag of knobs on the Third Night could never place.
Then it prints *know*. When Wick chose that *k*, all it could see was *y don't* and a space: eight characters. The
spots, the legs and the glass had slid out of its window long before, and after *don't*, parents in the pile
mostly write *know*. From there it wanders off, with no idea who it was talking about.

The counting machine had two words. Wick has eight letters. GPT-2 reads up to 1,024 pieces, a page or two, and
somehow keeps hold of the thread. How, you don't yet know.

<MathDrawer title="counting, Wick's voice, and temperature">
  **The counting machine.** With $c(a\,b\,w)$ the number of times words $a, b$ were followed by $w$ in the
  training messages, Wick picks the next word with probability

  $$
  P(w \mid a\,b) = \frac{c(a\,b\,w)}{\sum_{v} c(a\,b\,v)},
  $$

  and if the pair $a\,b$ never occurred it uses the last word alone, or, failing that, every word by its count
  (a crude *back-off*). Messages start and end with a mark, so "end of message" is one of the words it can pick.

  **Wick's voice.** The last eight characters $x_1 \dots x_8$ (from 42: a to z, digits, space, . , ' ? and a
  message boundary) are looked up in a table of 16 learned numbers each, $e(x)$, and joined into one list of 128.
  Then

  $$
  h = \tanh\!\big(W_1\,[e(x_1);\dots;e(x_8)] + b_1\big), \qquad z = W_2 h + b_2, \qquad
  p_i = \frac{e^{z_i / T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j / T}},
  $$

  with 256 hidden units, so $W_1$ is $256 \times 128$ and $W_2$ is $42 \times 256$: 44,490 knobs in all. $T$ is
  the temperature (training always uses $T = 1$). The wrongness for one example is $-\log p_y$ for the character
  $y$ that really came next. Each step averages its slope over 32 random positions in the training messages and
  updates every knob with Adam,<Cite k="kingma2014" /> using the paper's suggested $\beta_1 = 0.9$,
  $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$ and a step size of $0.005/(1 + t/3000)$ at step $t$. The random numbers
  come from a fixed seed, and the arithmetic is written so that it gives the same answer, to the last bit, on every
  device: your Wick is exactly the Wick in the book.

  **Temperature.** Dividing every score by $T$ before the softmax leaves the order of the guesses alone and
  changes how far apart they are: as $T \to 0$ all the probability goes to the top score, $T = 1$ is the model's
  own odds, and large $T$ flattens everything toward an even spread. GPT-2's bars use the same formula over its
  50,257 tokens.
</MathDrawer>
